# الطَبَقَة — "Stratum"

## A visual philosophy for plates from an imaginary discipline

**Stratum** holds that a capability is not a promise but a *specimen*. It can be laid on a dark
table, lit from one side, measured, indexed, and filed. The movement borrows the manners of the
anatomical plate and the instrument catalogue — the flat black ground of the specimen tray, the
hairline that means *measured*, the index number in the corner that means *one of a known set* —
and turns them on things that have no body at all: a reminder that sends itself, an answer that
arrives in a second, a month of hours that adds itself up. The result should read as a page torn
from a reference work that does not exist, printed once, on heavy stock, by someone who cared
more than the job required. Nothing here may look generated. Every alignment must look like the
last of many, the product of deep expertise and painstaking attention.

**Space and form.** The ground is near-black and unbroken, and it is the majority of the
composition — the specimen earns its place by being surrounded. A single hairline frame sits
inboard of the trim, and inside it everything obeys one modular grid whose gutters are visible
only as consistency. Form is reduced to the primitives of the drafting table: the true circle,
the arc of constant radius, the straight line, the small filled square used as a marker. No
shadow, no gradient, no gloss. Depth comes from stroke weight alone — three weights, never four —
and from the patience of repetition: lattices of identical marks, rows of identical ticks, a
curve sampled at regular intervals. The eye must find, on long looking, that the repetition was
not decoration but a measurement, and that it was counted correctly.

**Color and material.** Monochrome discipline: the black ground, two greys for the apparatus of
measurement, one warm off-white for what must be read. Then one colour — a single saturated
yellow — which is never given to type and never used twice in the same weight. Yellow is a
*fill*: it marks the one position in a lattice that matters, the one cell measured, the one arc
completed. It is the only warmth on the plate and so it carries the whole argument; spend it once
per composition, and the plate will feel authored rather than assembled. Material is implied
through restraint — the flatness of good ink on good paper, never the sheen of a screen.

**Scale and rhythm.** The typographic range is deliberately violent: one large word or phrase, at
a size that behaves as architecture rather than language, against markers small enough to demand
leaning in. Between those two extremes sits almost nothing. Right-to-left script is treated as
the structural element it is — its baseline anchors the page, its counters and ascenders are
composed like masonry, and the Latin apparatus (indices, units, set notation) stays subordinate,
monospaced, and quiet. Rhythm is established by the lattice and broken exactly once, by the
yellow, so the break registers as an event.

**Composition and balance.** Each plate is a cold upper field for the specimen and a warm lower
field for its identification, divided by a hairline that continues the grid. The figure is placed
off-centre with the confidence of a plate whose engraver knew the page would be trimmed, and the
negative space to one side is left genuinely empty — not filled, not textured, not softened.
Nothing overlaps; nothing touches the frame; nothing runs off the page. Margins are generous and
identical across the set, because a series is only a series if the eye can stack the plates from
memory.

**Visual hierarchy, and the series.** The order of discovery is fixed: the yellow mark, then the
figure, then the name, then the apparatus. Information lives in the drawing — a state is shown by
which cell is filled, a flow by which arc closes, a fault by which tick is missing — and the words
only name what the drawing has already proved. Across the series a single shared motif recurs on
every plate, identical in construction and different only in which position it marks, so that any
two plates side by side declare themselves members of one catalogue. That recurrence is the
craftsmanship the movement demands: mastery here is not invention per page but the discipline to
repeat a system exactly, at master-level execution, until the repetition itself becomes the
subject.
